Basic Structure Doctrine  |  GS 2 – Indian Polity  |  15 Marks  |  ~200-250 words
Introduction

The Basic Structure Doctrine, established in the landmark Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) case, holds that while Parliament can amend the Constitution under Article 368, it cannot alter or destroy its 'basic structure' — the fundamental features that give the Constitution its identity.

This doctrine has been both a guardian of constitutional values and a subject of debate regarding the balance of power between the judiciary and legislature.

Protection of Constitutional Essence
Safeguard against majoritarianism: Prevents elected governments from making sweeping amendments that could fundamentally alter the democratic character of India (e.g., preventing one-party rule or erosion of federalism).
Preserving fundamental rights: Struck down 39th Amendment (1975) that placed the PM's election beyond judicial review — protecting the right to equality and free elections as basic features.
NJAC Judgment (2015): Supreme Court struck down the National Judicial Appointments Commission as violating the independence of judiciary — a core basic structure element.
Federalism preserved: Minerva Mills (1980) reaffirmed limited amending power, preventing concentration of power at Centre at the expense of States.
Criticism: Judicial Overreach Concerns
Unelected body over Parliament: Critics argue that the SC — an unelected institution — overrides decisions of the elected legislature, undermining parliamentary sovereignty.
Undefined scope: 'Basic structure' has no exhaustive list in the Constitution — giving courts wide discretionary power that can lead to subjective interpretations.
NJAC controversy: Parliament unanimously passed the NJAC amendment — its nullification was seen by many as the judiciary protecting its own appointment process.
Democratic deficit: In a democracy, constitutional changes should ultimately reflect the popular will through elected representatives, not judicial veto.
Balanced View & Way Forward
1
Codification of Basic Structure
Parliament and judiciary should work towards identifying and codifying the 'basic features' through a constitutional amendment commission, bringing clarity and reducing judicial subjectivity.
2
Judicial Restraint
The judiciary must exercise restraint and avoid expanding the doctrine beyond its original intent — focusing on core constitutional values rather than policy matters.
3
Strengthening Collegium Transparency
The NJAC debate highlighted the need for a more transparent and accountable judicial appointment process while still preserving judicial independence.
Conclusion

The Basic Structure Doctrine is an essential constitutional safety net that prevents the Constitution from being hollowed out by transient parliamentary majorities. While concerns about judicial overreach are valid, the doctrine's existence has ensured that India remains a constitutional democracy with fundamental values intact.

The need of the hour is a collaborative constitutional culture where Parliament, Executive, and Judiciary each respect their domains — with the doctrine serving as the ultimate guardian, not an instrument of judicial supremacy.
